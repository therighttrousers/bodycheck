# 062: Background layers

Status: open. Still to settle: a cave's backdrop (§2.9, put off), the horizon behind a fade (§2.14, an option filed), the back wall's look (§3.1, to tune by eye), previewing decorations in the editor (§3.7), and the leanings in §2.3, §2.5 and §4.

## Context

Inciting event (2026-11-12): issue #202, background layers, which follows per-room environments (`061`). An environment's background is a procedural sky or a colour, and anything fancier was left for background layers (`061 §1.3`). Separately, the back wall with holes that `RoomNode` drew behind the tiles has been gone since `057`, and what replaces it is open (`057 §2.2`).

Goals:
- Background art under artists' control, made in the Godot editor. Nobody sets LDtk fields or paints tiles in LDtk for it: fields are abstract, and tiles fit non-tile visuals poorly (Neil).
- Art effort that a team of one, and kids working very part-time, can keep up with (Neil). A procedural background, as the back wall was, may be the better trade.

Tensions:
- Levels are authored in LDtk and read at runtime (`000 §1.2`), so the Godot editor shows nothing of a room. Art placed by hand needs a guide to where the tiles are, which may mean a build step, or reading LDtk in the editor.
- The camera is perspective. At `b` tiles behind the tiles' front faces, a screen shows about `15 (16.1 + b) / 16.1` tiles of height, and the layer moves at `16.1 / (16.1 + b)` of the tiles' speed: at `b = 16`, 30 by 40 tiles, at half speed. The camera boundary keeps outside space out of view only at the front faces (`042 §5.3`), so a deep layer shows past its room's edges, and from neighbouring rooms.
- Dithering in the colour pass may be on or off in the end; Neil hasn't decided (2026-11-12).

## 1. Two kinds of background

- [x] 1.1 What "background layers" covers. Claude: two different things.
  - **Backdrop:** far away, about the place's mood rather than particular tiles: sky, distant machinery, mountains, haze. It belongs to an environment.
  - **Set dressing:** close behind particular tiles: a pipe run behind this corridor, a window there. It belongs to a room.

  Depth decides between them. Deep layers placed per room would show from neighbouring rooms, shifted by parallax, and would have to cover about twice a room's area at `b = 16`. Shallow layers stay about inside their room, and what shows of a neighbour's through an opening is really there, so they need no fade. Decision (Neil): treat them separately.

## 2. Backdrops

- [x] 2.1 Where they live. Decision (Neil): in the environment's scene, which already brings along anything besides its `WorldEnvironment` and `Lighting` (`061 §1.1`), so a backdrop is made and tuned in the editor, and art scales with the number of environments, not of rooms.
- [x] 2.2 How they move. Decision (Neil, with Claude): a backdrop follows the camera with a parallax factor, rather than being placed in the world, so that it has no edges to hide and needs no guide. A node script on each layer does it, so the artist sets the factor in the editor.
- [~] 2.3 Render layer. Leaning: `Background`, as `041 §4.1` lists, so that the map's zoom and the debug views can leave backdrops out.
- [x] 2.4 Fading as environments blend. Each environment's scene stays in the tree while it has weight (`061 §4.1`), so its backdrop shows with it, and has to fade by the weight. Decision (Neil): not by a dissolve in the colour pass's Bayer pattern (`061 §5.1`): fading mustn't need dither, or change `mode_x.gdshader`, since whether dither stays on is undecided. Options:
  - A. Transparency: each layer's material takes the weight as its alpha. Overlapping backdrops of two environments sort by depth, or by render priority set per layer. Cheap; the colour pass then quantises the mix.
  - B. Fade into the mixed sky: each layer's colour moves toward the sky's by its weight, staying opaque. No sorting, and it reads as haze, but a heavier environment's opaque layer still hides a lighter one's, so the two backdrops pop where they overlap.
  - C. A wipe by position: each backdrop is cut where its rooms end, with a soft edge, like the weights themselves (`061 §3.1` E). Parallax makes "where its rooms end" hard to define for a layer at depth.
  - D. A swap where the weights cross a half. Free, but a pop.

  Claude leaned to A, which fades most like a crossfade and has no sorting problems among layers that are flat and parallel. Decision (Neil): A, transparency.
- [~] 2.5 What a layer is. Leaning: a textured quad, at a depth behind the tiles, for 2D art; or a model, for 3D. The quad suits pixel art and the placeholders (§2.6).
- [x] 2.6 Placeholder backdrops. They must be licensed so that we can use them, and be credited in `ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS.md`. Candidate sources (Claude): Kenney's background packs (CC0); OpenGameArt's parallax backgrounds, many CC0 or CC-BY; free packs on itch.io, whose licences vary pack by pack; Poly Haven's skies (CC0), which are panoramas and would need a sky layer rather than a quad. Or simple shapes made in Blender, which suit the look and need no credit. Decision (Neil, 2026-11-13): two packs from OpenGameArt, mountains and clouds for the outside, and a cave pack for later, credited in `ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS.md`.

- [x] 2.7 Parallax by depth (2026-11-13), refining §2.2. Each layer is a flat picture at a real depth behind the tiles, rather than moved by a factor set by hand, so the perspective camera gives the parallax, `16.1 / (16.1 + b)` of the tiles' speed at depth `b`, and layers take the environment's fog. A layer is a quad whose material maps the picture by world triplanar mapping, so the picture stays fixed in the world, and a script, `BackdropLayer`, moves the quad with the camera, across, up and down, or both, so that it stays in view: a horizon, such as mountains, follows only across. The layers are under a `Node3D` named `Backdrop` in the environment's scene. Decision (Neil): he built the outside's mountains this way.
- [x] 2.8 Sorting. Claude had expected parallel layers to sort by depth, since the camera never turns, but with a perspective camera Godot sorts transparent things by their straight-line distance from the camera (`render_forward_clustered.cpp`, `_fill_render_list`), so a near layer far above or below the view sorts behind a deeper one on the view's axis. Sorting by the transform's origin rather than the AABB's centre makes no difference for a quad. Options: render priorities set by hand on the materials, or each layer offsetting its sort by the difference between its distance and its depth along the view, each frame. Decision: the offset, which `BackdropLayer` sets, so nobody sets anything by hand.
- [ ] 2.9 What a cave's backdrop shows. Many parallax backgrounds are drawn for scrolling across only, with a ceiling and a floor, such as the dungeon placeholder's stalactites and stalagmites, but the camera can go any distance up or down as well, so nothing can hang from a ceiling or stand on a floor (Neil). Options (Claude): mostly darkness and fog, with a few faint features, seen through the back wall's holes, the back wall (§3) carrying the cave's shapes; art that tiles both ways with no up or down, such as rock, veins, roots and crystals; a strip repeated up and down, so that each repeat reads as another gallery; and ceilings and floors as room dressing (§4). Put off (Neil, 2026-11-15): it needs art, found or made.
- [x] 2.10 Filtering. Decision (Neil): linear, the material's default, for now, without lining texels up with screen pixels.

- [x] 2.11 Repeating rather than following (2026-11-15), superseding the axes of §2.7. Following the camera on an axis doesn't change what is seen, since the picture is fixed in the world: it only keeps the quad in view. What differs is whether the picture repeats along the axis. Neil found he wanted to follow on both axes, and couldn't place the mountains well in y. Decision (Neil): a layer says whether its picture repeats across and up and down, `RepeatX` and `RepeatY`; on a repeating axis the quad covers the view, sized from the camera's field of view, and moves with the camera, and on the other it keeps its authored place and size. A layer that repeats on neither, such as a single cloud, is an ordinary quad.
- [x] 2.12 Local UVs. World triplanar mapping needed very small UV scales, which the inspector rounds to two significant digits, and the scale and offset had to be worked out again, or found by eye, whenever a quad moved or was resized (Neil). Decision (Neil): a layer is authored as an ordinary quad, with the mesh's UVs, placed and sized to show one copy of the picture; in the game, `BackdropLayer` takes copies of its mesh and material, turns triplanar mapping off and sets the UVs' scale and offset each frame, so the copy stays where it was authored, repeated along the repeating axes. Quads that don't cover the view are kept, for clouds. Repeating the quads themselves, rather than the texture, perhaps with models, is left until it's needed.
- [x] 2.13 The backdrop's anchor. The scene's origin was the world's, which is at the lowest and leftmost room's edges, so adding a room far below moved the outside's horizon (Neil). Options: the centre of the box around all the environment's rooms, set once at load; an anchor per room, blended by the rooms' weights, which slides the backdrop across the blend band; a crossfade between copies; or moving the anchor only on entering the environment, which keeps state from frame to frame, against `061 §3.1`. Leaning (Neil, with Claude): the shared box's centre, to start with. Rooms of an environment far apart, such as outside rooms near the map's top, can use an environment of their own, an inherited scene. Decision (Neil, 2026-11-15): the shared box's centre is good enough for now; whether anything more elaborate is wanted is an open question.
- [ ] 2.14 The horizon behind a fading backdrop. While a backdrop fades, the sky's horizon shows through it. To fix last in the PR (Neil). Options: render each environment to a texture and combine them; or fade only the lighter environment's backdrop, with the heavier one opaque behind it. Tried (2026-11-15): each backdrop as opaque as its weight relative to the heaviest's, so the heaviest is opaque. Rejected (Neil): the horizon shows when the departing environment's weight is low, not the arriving one's; relative to the lightest's instead, the lightest pops out rather than fading; softer minimums and maximums aren't worth pursuing. Also rejected (Neil): an opaque deepest layer in each backdrop, which wouldn't help, and a sky without a horizon. Filed as an option (Neil, 2026-11-15), too complicated for now: render each environment's backdrop, in front of its own sky, into a viewport of its own, with a camera that copies the game's, and draw the images mixed by the weights behind the world, such as by a sky shader. Each environment could then have any sky, since skies would no longer be mixed property by property (`061 §1.3`). Rendering depth as well, written by the compositing shader, would keep features that read depth, such as fog, working, with the fog's settings mixed as now. The cost to run is an extra pass at low resolution per environment in a blend; the cost to build is keeping the cameras in step within a frame, following the resolution switch, and the compositing. Meanwhile (Neil): transitional environments, or geometry that hides the sky and the backdrops between environments.

- [x] 2.15 A line along an edge that doesn't repeat (2026-11-15). The texture must repeat, and the mountains' bottom row is opaque, so with nearest filtering a line popped in and out along the quad's top edge: pixels on the edge whose V lands a hair below 0 wrap to the bottom row. Options: pull the UVs in by half a texel on the axes that don't repeat; shrink the quad by part of a pixel; a shader that repeats on one axis and clamps on the other, which would replace `StandardMaterial3D`; or the art, with a transparent row below the opaque one. Decision (Neil): the half-texel inset, which fixes it for every picture, at the cost of a stretch of one texel across the quad. Also (Neil): `RepeatY` is on by default, as layers usually repeat both ways.

## 3. The back wall

- [~] 3.1 What replaces it (`057 §2.2`). Decision (Neil): procedural, as the old back wall was, spreading from the solid tiles with holes in it, so that no room needs art of its own. To decide: its parameters, such as its mesh or material, density and depth. Refined (Neil, 2026-11-15): it spreads only from `Ground` tiles, since a back wall of grass makes no sense, and not in every environment: none outside (but see §3.8), and perhaps none with holes in an industrial one. Its parameters are §3.5's; their values are the old wall's, to tune by eye: it starts as the `Ground` tiles and grows 3 times, each tile into each neighbour with a chance of 0.4, staying in the room, drawn with the ground's mesh darkened by 0.6, each tile 1 deep and from 1 to 1.75 behind the tiles' front layer, in four steps, and turned by a random quarter-turn. The old wall's 0.45 was a threshold on a sum of sines, which isn't uniform, not a chance.
- [x] 3.2 Where its parameters live. Decision (Neil): in each environment's scene, so that an artist tunes them in the editor, and a cave's wall can differ from a facility's.
- [x] 3.3 When. Decision (Neil, 2026-11-15): now, issue #208.
- [x] 3.4 Decorations per environment (Neil, 2026-11-15). The back wall is one case of what an environment adds to each of its rooms, procedurally: a cave could add stalactites and stalagmites, in front of the tiles as well as behind, which don't belong outside. Decision (Neil): the generators live in the game, not in Core, in a directory of their own (for now `game/decoration/`, until issue #210 reorganizes `game/`), and `RoomNode` gets what they make through `IAssetLibrary`, for each room, from the room's environment. Claude: a generator is a decorator, a `Resource` whose exported properties are its settings, and an environment's scene lists its decorators in a node named `Decorations`, which only holds them; the asset library reads each environment's list once, and asks each decorator for its room's decoration.
- [x] 3.5 The back wall's settings, as a decorator's (§3.4). Decision (Claude, with Neil): the kind of tile it spreads from, the kind whose mesh draws it, how many times it grows, the chance of growing, how much darker it is, and its depth and unevenness. A chance of 1 leaves no holes near the tiles, for an industrial wall.
- [x] 3.6 What a decorator may depend on (Neil, with Claude, 2026-11-15). So that a decorator can run in the editor later (§3.7) at little cost now: it is pure and deterministic, depending only on the room, its settings and the asset library's meshes, never the scene tree or the game's state; it takes Core's `IAssetLibrary`, or a smaller interface extracted from it, then or now, rather than the game's library; and what it makes is in the room's coordinates and built of the engine's own nodes. Its random numbers are seeded by the room's name and the tile's place in the room, not in the world, so that moving a level in LDtk keeps its decoration, and anything placed by hand around it (§4.2).
- [~] 3.7 Previewing decorations in the editor. What "tweaking the output" means: Decision (Neil): tuning the decorators' settings, and adding models on top in a room's scene (§4.2), never editing what a decorator makes, which would need baking it into the scene, and then regenerating would fight hand edits. Little decorating can't be done with both (Neil). Leaning (Neil): the decorators become `[Tool]` scripts, and the room's scene shows a preview of its decorations beside the guide of §4.3 B. Not now: decorating comes near the end of development. Changing a room's layout a lot then is hard, even if no work is lost (Neil).
- [x] 3.8 A back wall outside (Neil, 2026-11-15), refining §3.1. The wall grows only into empty tiles, so that a wall spreading from `Ground` stays out from behind the grass, and the outside has one too, which grows with a chance of 0.8. Rationale (Neil): to cover the transition between the outside and the dark cave, so that most of the environments' crossfade happens while the player can't see the sky.

## 4. Set dressing placed by hand

- [x] 4.1 When. Decision (Neil): later, when a particular room needs more than the back wall gives, such as for a story beat. Until then, backdrops (§2) and the back wall (§3) are the background.
- [~] 4.2 Where it lives. Leaning (Neil agrees, 2026-11-15): an optional scene per room, such as `assets/rooms/<world>/<level>.tscn`, which the game adds at the room's anchor, so that moving a level in LDtk takes its dressing along. With the anchor at the room's bottom-left, growing a level right or up leaves the dressing in place, but growing it left or down shifts it, which nothing detects. A room's scene could also turn off the back wall for its room.
- [~] 4.3 The guide in the editor. Options:
  - A. LDtk's own image export: LDtk writes a PNG of each level when it saves, and a small GDScript tool node in the room's scene shows it on a quad at the tiles' plane, the level's size, at the room's origin. No build step, and no Core in the editor. Flat, but the solid tiles' silhouette is most of what placement needs. To check: LDtk's image export setting and what it writes.
  - B. A C# tool node that reads `main.ldtk` with `LdtkReader` and draws the tiles as `RoomNode` does, at their real depth, with a preview camera at the game's distance, and reads the file again when it changes. To check, with a spike: whether the editor unloads the game's assembly cleanly after a build, since libraries that cache types, possibly `System.Text.Json`, can stop it, and the editor then needs restarting.
  - C. A build step: an MSBuild target, after the build, writes the rooms as Godot scenes, so that the editor's build button rebuilds them. It means writing Godot's scene format by hand, running Core outside the engine, and generated files that either come out the same on every build, for CI's no-changes check, or are ignored by Git.

  Leaning (Claude): A first; B if placement needs the tiles' depth; C only if both fall short. Leaning (Neil, 2026-11-15): B, which can also show the room's decorations (§3.7).
