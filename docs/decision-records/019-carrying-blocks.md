# 019: Carrying blocks on walkers

## Context

Inciting event (2026-10-20): issue #22, "Allow blocks to be carried", taken up as one of the last changes before a playtester is onboarded.

Closed on 2026-10-20. Outcomes are in the design docs: [movement.md](../designs/movement.md), with what is still open in its open questions, [architecture.md](../designs/architecture.md) and [models.md](../designs/models.md).

Picking a block up is already easy: phase down into it from above or from the side, and it ends up on the player's head. It doesn't stay there. It stays on platforms and other blocks.

Cause: where two bodies touch, the engine uses the smaller of their coefficients of friction (no body is `Rough`, `001 §1.4`). A walker's is 0 (`005 §3.8`), so every contact it has slides freely. At its feet, traction stands in for friction. At its head, nothing does. A block's friction of 1 is thrown away on the one contact it is carried by. Godot exposes neither a per-contact friction callback nor friction per shape, so the walker can't have a grippy top alone.

Neil's position: a controller on the block that applies friction as traction while it stands on a walker feels wrong. Blocks aren't controlled by anything, and future simple props shouldn't need controllers. That suggests a solution in `Entity` or `WorldNode`.

Goals:
- A block carried by a walker moves with it, as one carried by a platform does.
- No controller on props.

Tensions:
- `005 §3.8` decided that the base `Entity` has no motors, and is what a block is.
- Neil would rather not write lifetime-management code (`005 §3.8`): a joint should live exactly as long as what uses it.

## 1. Where the grip lives

- [x] 1.1 Who grips the carried body? Decision (Neil): (d). Options:
  - (a) A controller on the block, applying traction as friction while it stands on a walker. Against: blocks aren't controlled (Neil), and the block isn't acting, only being held.
  - (b) `WorldNode` finds the pairs of an entity and the walker under it each tick, and grips them. Against: it puts a rule of bodies in Simulation, which its `intent.md` excludes, and `WorldNode` would manage the joints' lifetimes.
  - (c) The walker grips what stands on it, the mirror of traction, which grips what it stands on. Against: a walker has any number of riders, so it needs a pool of joints that come and go with its contacts.
  - (d) Every entity grips its support. An entity has at most one `Support`, so one joint, a child of the entity, with node A the support: the structure of the traction joint. Its X motor, with a target speed of 0 relative to the support and a force limit of a coefficient of friction times the normal force on that contact, is Coulomb friction: it holds until the push exceeds the limit, then slides.
  - Rationale (Claude): no controller is involved, it covers anything with friction that ends up on a walker, and the joint lives as long as its entity. It breaks the letter of `005 §3.8`, but keeps its reasons: the lifetimes match, and the rule that an entity switches off every motor before its controller runs covers this motor too.
- [x] 1.2 One joint or two? Decision (Neil): one. The base `Entity` owns the support joint, and the traction motor moves there from `WalkerEntity`. With two, a walker could be under two motors on the same axis against the same support. This supersedes part of `005 §3.8`: every entity has a motor.
- [x] 1.3 Decision (Neil): (c), worked out in 1.6. Unifying the two `ApplyRun`s is put off (Neil): he wants to think first about what adding a new kind of walker should be like. How does a walker's controller drive, when its entity already applies friction? Neil: `ApplyTraction` may change, to assume that friction is dealt with by `Entity`, though that may not be where we end up. Today both controllers call `ApplyTraction(sign(move) × topSpeed, drive + friction)`, where friction is `GroundFriction × Weight` when idle or reversing and 0 otherwise. Idle, that is a target of 0 with a limit of μN: exactly the base entity's friction, with the walker's ground friction as μ. A joint axis has one motor, with one target and one limit, so drive and friction can't be two motors added together. Sketches:
  - (a) The controller's traction replaces the entity's friction for the tick, as now. An idle walker then just doesn't call it. The controller still adds friction in when reversing.
  - (b) The controller asks for drive only, and the entity merges it with friction: the target is the drive's, and the limit is the drive plus the friction when the drive opposes the motion, or the drive alone when it doesn't. The rule "driving the way you're going gets no friction" moves into `Entity`, for every kind of body.
  - (c) The controller only adds to what the entity does, and corrects for its friction where the rule says there is none. Neil weakly prefers controllers that add to what `Entity` does over ones that replace it, and would keep the rule in the controllers. Duplicating the logic of `ApplyTraction`'s callers between `PlayerController.ApplyRun` and `RobotController.ApplyRun` doesn't matter here (Neil): unifying them has been on his mind anyway.
  - Seen in passing: when reversing, the target is the far top speed and the limit includes friction, so for the rest of the tick friction keeps pushing past zero. It lasts at most a tick, and is in the code today.
- [x] 1.6 What does "adding" mean for one motor? Decision (Neil, agreeing with Claude): as leaning. Two motors on one axis really do add: the solver finds a force for each. One motor has one target and one limit, so adding needs a rule. Leaning (Claude):
  - On the ground, `Entity` sets its friction, a target of 0 and a limit of the friction force F, and exposes F. The controller's traction adds force to the limit, and its target becomes the motor's: `ApplyTraction(targetSpeed, extraForce)` gives a target of `targetSpeed` and a limit of F + `extraForce`, with the motor off when that is 0 or less. Only the force adds. The target is replaced, since there is only one, but the entity's target is 0, so an idle walker that calls nothing brakes as it does now.
  - Each case of today's controllers, with F their ground friction times their weight: idle, no call; reversing, an extra force of the drive; driving the way it is going, an extra force of the drive minus F, which may be negative and which corrects for the friction. All three give exactly the motor of today. So does driving above top speed, where the drive is 0 and the motor is off.
  - Rejected: a second joint for the drive, with two motors that add in the solver. They do add exactly, but a controller can't cancel the friction motor: while moving it pushes back with all of F, so a forward drive would need F more, and would then hold its speed against pushes with F more as well.
- [x] 1.4 Where does each coefficient of friction come from, and how do two combine? Decision (Neil, 2026-10-20): (c), below. Put off to the end of this PR (Neil): whether a controller should be what tunes a walker's ground and carrying friction, which it does only if it is expected to change them in reaction to its surroundings. Put off (Neil), to be taken up again before this PR is finished. Until then, the motor uses the rider's own coefficient, and ignores the support's. Neil expects friction to be what simulates a walker grabbing its rider, so a walker's head probably needs more than a block's 1. The walker's 0 was only ever needed on its sides (gripping walls) and for landing (losing speed), and the motor fixes landing for walkers anyway, since traction's limit comes from tuning, not from the contact's impulse (`001 §2.1`). With the support's friction ours, a walker's head can grip.
  - Neil, spitballing: bodies get a `BottomFriction` and a `TopFriction`, with the bottom treated as rough, so that the larger of the two wins.
  - Claude: with the larger winning, ice (a low top) can't make a block (a high bottom) slide. Once a walker's top no longer has to be 0, the usual rules work: the smaller wins, or the product, with a walker's top at 1. Only the sides need to stay frictionless, and they keep the engine's friction.
  - Today a walker's ground friction is a feature (`PlayerGroundFriction`, `RobotGroundFriction`, 4), and a block's and a platform's friction is in `BodyTuning`. Whether the walker's becomes a body value with the rest is part of this.
  - Taken up again on 2026-10-20, after 4.7. How much grip a walker's head needs, for the player carrying a block of mass 2 (the player's is 1), with gravity of 28.1 tiles per second squared:
    - Driving forward from rest: the drive, 67.5, accelerates all three units of mass at 22.5, which friction of 0.8 holds. The block that slipped in `wobble` and `carry` slipped because of 4.8, not because of the drive.
    - Braking, idle: the player's friction, 4 times the load, decelerates both at 4 g. Holding the block needs a coefficient of 4: as much as the walker's own grip on the ground.
    - Reversing: friction and the reverse drive, 135, decelerate both at 5.6 g, which needs 5.6.
    - So with the rider's own 1, a block on a running player's head slides off forward whenever the player stops or turns. Any coefficient above the walker's own ground friction plus its reverse drive per unit of what it carries holds it always.
  - Options:
    - (a) As now: the rider's own coefficient, 1 for a block. A walker's head is slippery compared with its feet; carried blocks slide off when the walker stops.
    - (b) Neil's spitball: a body's top and bottom have coefficients of their own, and a surface may be rough, in which case the larger of two wins, as in Godot. A walker's top is rough, so the walker sets the grip on whatever it carries: the carrier grabs. Everything else stays smooth, so later surfaces like ice still lower friction under blocks and walkers alike. A walker's top coefficient is a feature, like its ground friction, read through its controller, since it is tuning that playtesting will want to change (`PlayerCarryFriction`, `RobotCarryFriction`).
    - (c) (b) without coefficients for tops in general: only a walker's head has one, and it always wins. Less general; the same today.
    - (d) The product of the two coefficients, with a walker's top above 1. Real materials don't combine this way, but it is common in games. Ice under a walker's top would need care: a high head times a low bottom.
  - Leaning (Claude): (b), or (c) to start. A rough head is the "grabbing" Neil expects, and it names who decides: the carrier.
- [x] 1.5 Does the motor replace the engine's friction on supports, or add to it? Decision (Neil): (a), so that blocks keep their friction against walls, and for performance. If (a) turns out too complex, try (b). On a block on a platform, the engine already gives friction 1. Options:
  - (a) The motor adds only what the engine's combining rule dropped: on when the support's engine friction is less than the entity's grip. The engine keeps every contact it handles well; the motor is on only for riders of walkers, and its joint moves less.
  - (b) Every entity's friction with its support is the motor's, and the engine's friction is 0 on every entity. One source of friction on supports, and one rule for combining. Costs: a motor on every resting body, and entities lose friction against walls and against each other's sides, e.g. a block wedged between two walls slides down.

## 2. Details of the motor

- [~] 2.1 The normal force. Decision (Neil): (e), to try first. Options:
  - (a) `Weight`. Simple, but a block carrying another block would grip with its own weight only while moving both, and a block landing on a running player decelerates at only μg relative to it, so it can slide off.
  - (b) The floor contacts' impulses with the support, from `_IntegrateForces`, divided by the step's length. What the engine's friction would use: right for stacks, and on landing (Neil's question) the impact's impulse gives the next tick a large limit, so a landing block settles at once instead of sliding. As of the last step, so the tick of landing itself has no grip. Bounce is 0 everywhere, so there is no bounce to settle. A walker's traction keeps its limit from tuning, so the landing problem of `001 §2.1` doesn't come back.
  - Leaning (Claude): (b), if the engine reports the impulses well enough (2.3). It doesn't, so (b) is out.
  - Options left, after 2.3:
    - (a) `Weight`, as above.
    - (c) The load: the entity's weight plus the loads of the entities whose support it is, from each entity's `Support` as of the last step. Found from the entity's ceiling contacts with other entities (Neil: agreed; it could be expensive, but nothing will carry large stacks. A cap on the load can come if it is ever needed, and not before). Right for stacks at rest, which is what carrying is; it leaves out vertical acceleration, such as a jump's thrust or a landing.
    - (d) (c), plus the impact on the tick after a landing, measured by the entity itself: its mass times the change in its vertical velocity relative to its support over the landing step, plus its weight. 2.3 found the engine's estimate of an impact a third of its size, so it is no use for this. The entity has to keep the last tick's velocity, as history, not as a copy of what the engine holds.
    - (e) (c), with no limit on the tick after a landing: whatever lands stops slipping at once. Needs no history: the tick after a landing is the tick the joint is anchored to a new support.
  - What Neil cares about in impacts (2026-10-20):
    - G1. A walker that runs off a ledge and keeps driving the same way isn't slowed by landing. Slowing a walker that isn't driving is fine, and may help players stick tough landings.
    - G2. A block that lands on a walker's head stays there about when real physics says it would, or more often.
  - G1, for every option: a walker driving the way it is going adds the drive minus F (1.6), so its limit is the drive whatever F is. That needs the controller to read the F that the entity applies in the same tick, which 1.6 already has it expose. An idle walker that lands stops dead under (d) and (e), and brakes as now under (a) and (c). A walker that lands while reversing is the catch: its target is top speed the other way, and its limit the drive plus F, so with an impact in F it reaches full speed backwards in one tick. Its controller would have to add less, e.g. only as much of F as ordinary friction would be, which is controller logic Neil doesn't mind duplicating (1.3).
  - G2: by real physics, the impact's friction can take up to the friction coefficient times the impact's impulse off the slip. A block falling 2.2 tiles hits at 10.9 tiles per second, so with friction 1, the impact alone stops a slip of up to about 10.9; past that, the rest slides off at μg. Onto a player at top speed (15), it slides about 0.3 tiles more and stays on.
    - (a) and (c) fail it: with no impact term, a slip of 15 slides about 4 tiles, off the head.
    - (d) meets it about as physics says, a tick late: in the landing step the walker's contact has no friction, so the block slips for a tick, about 0.25 tiles at 15, before the motor acts.
    - (e) meets it by the "or more often" clause: a landing block always stops slipping, however fast the walker runs.
  - Between (d) and (e): (d) keeps blocks that land on a sprinting player able to slide off when physics says so, which puzzles could use; (e) is simpler.
  - Decision (Neil): try (e) first, and see whether it feels wrong. He expects it won't: when a block lands on a character moving fast and doesn't slip off, players will take it as intended, the character catching it.
- [x] 2.2 Where the joint is, on a body that rotates (Neil). Decision (Neil): (c), after (b) (4.3); first, try (b). If (c) is easy to add after that, Neil may try it even if (b) doesn't make blocks misbehave. A joint's place in each body is fixed in that body's frame when Godot builds the engine's constraint, which it does when node A changes. The joint is a child of the entity, so its point on the entity turns with it: put at a block's base, it is on a side face after a quarter turn.
  - How the motor acts, read from the sources on 2026-10-20 (Jolt's `SixDOFConstraint.cpp`, Godot's `jolt_generic_6dof_joint_3d.cpp`). Godot's body A is Jolt's body 1, and body B is body 2; with no node A, body 1 is fixed to the world. The motor's axes turn with body A. Its push acts on both bodies at body B's point, where it is now: the push back on A is not at a point fixed in A. The linear target velocity is passed on unchanged: B's speed relative to A. A motor's settings change the constraint in place; only limits and their flags rebuild it.
  - Accurate friction acts along the surface that is touched, at the point that touches it. So A should be the body whose face is touched, which gives the axis, and B the body whose point touches it, ideally one that doesn't turn, which gives the point.
  - That explains the walker's joint (PR #53, which fixed issue #48). A walker's feet touch its support's face, and a walker doesn't turn, so A is the support and B the walker, with the joint at the feet: on a tipped block, the axis lies along the block's top face, and the push is at the feet, exactly. Before #53, the joint was at the walker's center, a lever arm above the block's surface, which tipped it.
  - Why swapping A and B on the walker's joint didn't work, as far as can be told: the axis would then be the walker's, level while the block tips, and the point would be fixed in the block, turning away from the feet as it tipped and as the walker walked along it. The target velocity's sign also flips: it is A's speed relative to B then.
  - A block carried by a walker, flat, as it is while carried: every option below is exact. Tipped by an angle θ, it rests on its lower corner, on the walker's head:
    - (a) At the block's center, A the walker. The axis is level and right; the push is at the block's center, half its height too high, at any angle. Least accurate.
    - (b) At the contact when anchored, A the walker, B the block: the entity moves the joint there before setting node A. The axis is level and right. The point turns with the block, so tipped it is above the corner that touches, by the block's half-width times sin θ.
    - (c) As (b), and anchored again when the point has moved off the contact by more than some distance. Most accurate, by as much as the threshold allows, and the constraint is rebuilt more often (3.3).
    - (d) Swapped (Neil): A the block, B the walker, with the joint at the top of the walker's head. The point is right at any angle, since the walker doesn't turn. The axis turns with the block, so tipped it is off the surface by θ, and a push of F has a vertical part of F sin θ.
  - Neither (b) nor (d) is exact while the block tips; each is wrong by an amount proportional to the tilt for small angles, (b) in where it pushes and (d) in which way. A carried block is flat, and a tipped one leaves soon after, so the difference lasts a short time.
  - Leaning (Claude): (b). As accurate as (d), and it keeps one rule for every joint, traction's included: B is the entity that owns the joint, A its support, and the joint is at the contact when anchored. (d) would make the rule depend on which of the two is a walker, and flip the target's sign. If tipped blocks behave badly on heads, try (c).
  - Checked (2026-10-20, `--probe=joint-placement`, new): Godot takes the joint node's current place when node A changes, and not otherwise. A motor pushes a weightless block along X, and the block's spin shows where: none with the joint at its center; still none after moving the joint to its base with node A unchanged; then 0.62 radians per second, counterclockwise, as a push below the center gives, after setting node A to another body, and the same after setting it back to the world. So (b) works by moving the joint, then setting node A. Moving the joint without a new node A does nothing.
  - What counts as a change (Neil): a new value, compared as paths, not as objects. With the joint moved to the center, setting node A to a newly made path to the same body, to the same path object as before, or to its own value, all leave the push where it was. Clearing node A and setting it again in the same tick moves it. So today's `ApplyTraction`, which sets node A only when the support differs, rebuilds only then, and (c) would have to clear and set node A to re-anchor on the same support.
- [x] 2.3 Does Godot's Jolt module report contact impulses well enough to limit a motor with? Outcome: no. Measured on 2026-10-20 with `--probe=carry` (new, in the "Carrying" room), from each body's direct state, as the sum of its floor contacts' impulses along the normal over the tick's length:
  - A block resting on tiles: exactly 1.000 weights, steady. But a second block is stacked on it, so the true figure is 2.
  - The block stacked on it: 0.000 weights.
  - A block landing on the idle player's head: 7.70 weights on the tick of impact, 0.03 the next, then 0.000 for as long as it rests there, from both bodies' sides. From the block's change in velocity (from -10.855 tiles per second to about 0), the contact really pushed 24.1 weights in that step: the estimate is a third of it.
  - So the figure is an estimate of each contact on its own, not what the solver applied: one body falling for a tick onto a static one gives exactly its weight, two resting bodies falling together give nothing, and an impact gives too little. It is right only for one body on tiles, which is the case that doesn't need the motor.
  - Also seen, without any motor yet: when the player starts running, the block slides at 6 tiles per second relative to it within 5 ticks, and is off its head within 10.
  - Consequence: 2.1 (b) is out.

## 3. Consequences

- [x] 3.1 Carrying costs the carrier. Decided in 6.4: less than physics says, since the drive grows with the load. First leaning (Neil): roughly what physics says it should cost. Before the player can phase, the cost can limit puzzles; phasing lowers it greatly, since blocks can then be thrown.
  - Claude: physically, a loaded walker also grips harder, but traction's limit comes from tuning, not from the load (an open question in [movement.md](../designs/movement.md)). As it stands, a carried block makes the player accelerate and brake by more than the masses alone would say. For the cost to be roughly physical, traction's limit may need to grow with the load. Braking would, with 2.1 (c), (d) or (e), since the walker's friction would come from its load.
  - Put off (Neil): whether loaded walkers should grip harder is decided at the end of this PR, and then whether it stays an open question.
- [x] 3.2 Checked 2026-10-20 with `--probe=carry-checks` (new): moved 3 tiles through entities, as a phase moves it, the player leaves the block where it was, and it falls straight down to the floor, with no jerk from its joints, still anchored to the player. To be checked: a joint anchored to a walker that phases, and so moves through entities before the engine's step (`015 §5.21`). With every limit off it constrains nothing, so it shouldn't snap.
- [x] 3.3 Checked 2026-10-20 with `--probe=carry-checks`: a walker freed under a block causes no error; the block's grip is off from the next tick, and it falls. Building a joint again by changing its node A takes about 17 microseconds. To be checked, as for traction (`005 §1.4`): what moving the joint costs for a body that changes support every few ticks, and what happens to it when its support is freed.
- [ ] 3.4 What does a motor cost? (Neil: if motors are expensive, a block shouldn't always have one.) Put off (Neil): 1.5 (a) already keeps motors to the bodies that need them, for friction against walls. Not measured. Claude's estimate, from how Jolt solves constraints: a six-degree-of-freedom constraint with every axis free and one motor costs one row in the solver per iteration. A box resting on another has a contact manifold of up to four points, each with a normal row and two friction rows, so one motor is about a twelfth of the work of a resting box's contacts. The cost more likely to matter is moving the joint, since Godot rebuilds the engine's constraint when node A changes (3.3). To be measured with a probe: many blocks resting and carried, with the motor always on and only when needed.

## 4. The first draft

Built by Claude on 2026-10-20, on the decisions above, before Neil has read it.

- [x] 4.1 As built.
  - `Entity` owns the joint: its node B is set when it is first anchored, and node A whenever the support changes, after the joint is moved to the middle of the contacts with what the entity stands on, level (2.2).
  - Each tick, `Step` switches the motor off, applies friction, runs the controller and applies drag. Friction is on when the entity is on the ground and the engine's friction, the smaller of the two bodies' coefficients, falls short of the entity's own coefficient: a target of 0 and a limit of the shortfall times the load (1.5 (a), 1.4 for now, 2.1 (c)). On the tick after a landing, `Landed`, it adds as much force as stops the slip within the tick (2.1 (e)). Finite, so that a controller's correction of it stays exact in single precision.
  - The support's coefficient is read from the engine (`PhysicsServer3D.BodyGetParam`), from the collider stood on, so tiles need nothing from Simulation.
  - The load comes from the entities in contact from above whose `Support` is this entity, recursively.
  - `Friction` is the limit applied. `WalkerEntity.ApplyTraction(targetSpeed, extraForce)` sets the target and a limit of `Friction` plus the extra (1.6). Both controllers call it only when driving: an extra of the drive when reversing, the drive minus `Friction` otherwise.
  - A walker's coefficient comes from its controller, as the new default interface member `ICharacterController.GroundFriction`, which the player's and the robot's controllers read from their features each tick. Other bodies use their `BodyTuning.EngineFriction`.
- [x] 4.2 Two rules found while making the tests pass.
  - A walker whose controller doesn't run this tick has no friction: its friction is part of its control. Tests that switch controllers off to coast a walker need it, and so did the walker before, which had no traction then.
  - An entity that has been asked to move through entities has no friction until the engine's next step, since what it stands on is from before the move. The player's controller already skips its run on that tick (`015 §5.21`); without the rule, friction braked a phase's throw (`PhaseKeepsTheCenterOfMassAndThrowsTheTargetBack`).
- [x] 4.3 2.2 (b) and 2.1 (e) clash. Decision (Neil): the proposal below: 2.2 (c), with the catch at the center. A block dropped onto a player running at top speed, landing at 5 tiles per second slower than it, was caught by (e) at its base, which set it spinning: it tipped and fell off. With the joint at the center of a body that rotates, 2.2 (a), the catch is clean: both bodies end at 7.59 tiles per second, which is their momentum shared exactly, and the block turns by under 0.03 radians. The draft has (a) for bodies that rotate and (b) for the rest, which keeps walkers' joints at the feet, until Neil decides. Options: (i) as the draft; (ii) (b), and let a landing catch tip blocks, as real friction would; (iii) (b), with the catch's part of the limit acting at the center, which would need a second joint.
  - Found by Neil, playing (2026-10-20): a block carried while running wobbles, and one that lands partly off the head hangs over the edge and rocks, instead of falling off. Reproduced with `--probe=wobble` and `--probe=offset-landing` (new, in the "Carrying" room).
    - `offset-landing`: a block dropped 1.2 tiles onto the idle player's head, its center 0.5 tiles to one side, which is 0.1 past the head's edge. It tips to 0.76 radians, swings back to flat, and tips again, every half second or so, with its center rising and falling, and its center never moves more than 0.07 tiles further out.
    - `wobble`: the block slips back 0.45 tiles as the player starts running, since friction of 1 can't hold it to the player's acceleration (1.4), which leaves its center past the head's back edge. It tips back by up to 0.2 radians and stays there, held, until the player stops; then it slides forward to 0.23 tiles ahead of the player's center and settles flat.
    - Cause: with the joint at the center, the motor holds the center's horizontal speed to the walker's. A block tipping off an edge turns about the corner it rests on, which doesn't slip, while its center moves outward; the motor resists the center's motion, so the block hangs from it like a pendulum. Real friction acts at the corner and doesn't resist tipping.
    - So 2.2 (a) is wrong for ordinary friction, and the contact is right, but only the contact as it is now: (c). The catch at the base tipped the block because it stood in for an impact's friction without the impact: in a real landing, the friction's impulse comes with the normal's, whose spread across the face balances its torque, and a square block with friction of 1 or less doesn't tip from it. Our catch comes a tick after the impact, against nothing but the block's weight.
    - Proposal (Claude): (c) for ordinary friction, with the joint anchored again whenever the contact has moved off it by more than some distance, in the entity's frame; and on the tick of a landing's catch, the joint at the center of a body that rotates, which is (iii) with one joint moved twice instead of two. Anchoring again on the same support needs node A cleared and set (2.2). Each landing then rebuilds the joint twice, and a body tipping on an edge rebuilds it every few ticks (3.3).
- [x] 4.5 Friction when a walker's controller doesn't run (Neil): Decision (Neil): (c). counterintuitive, since `Entity` applies friction. The controller doesn't run only when `WorldNode.ControllersEnabled` is off, which only tests and probes use, to coast walkers; pausing skips `Step` altogether. Options:
  - (a) As the draft: a walker's coefficient is 0 when its controller doesn't run, through a parameter of `GroundFriction`.
  - (b) An entity that isn't controlled has no grip at all, whatever its kind: only the engine acts on it, as `Step`'s documentation already says ("it only coasts, under drag and gravity").
