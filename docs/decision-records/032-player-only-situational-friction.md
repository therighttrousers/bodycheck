# 032: Player-only situational friction

## Context

Inciting event (2026-10-26): with issue #54, robots turn around when they stall, and don't drive towards a side they touch, so a robot boxed in stands still. Neil found two problems:

- The player takes a long time to run off an idle robot. Driving, the player slips: its coefficient is 0, not rough. But an idle robot has its ground coefficient, 4, rough, and a rough coefficient wins (`027 §1.4`, `§1.5`), so the player's traction fights friction of 4. A driving robot slips too, which is why this didn't show while robots never stood still.
- A block on a robot's head has the robot's carrying coefficient, 6, rough.

Neil: situational friction in `Entity.SetFrictionAfterEntitySteps` isn't necessarily wrong, but it is misapplied. Friction will be wrong in some situation whatever we do, so keep the situations to one entity, the player, which has the most reason to need them. A body's coefficient is the same for all its contacts, so what one body's friction does depends on every body it touches; the fewer bodies whose friction changes, the fewer combinations to get wrong.

Goals:
- Only the player's friction changes with its situation. Every other body has one coefficient, its own, all the time.
- Robots behave about as they do now, retuned.

Anti-goals: choosing the coefficients. Neil is considering lowering friction in general, from playing, and that is for playtesting.

## 1. Who has situational friction

- [x] 1.1 Decision (Neil): only the player.
  - Only the player carries: a body standing on anything else has its own coefficient. `CarryFriction` goes from robots; whether it stays for the player is §2. Superseded by §1.7.
  - Only the player slips while it drives, and only the player has no friction in the air, so that it slides along walls (`027 §1.2`, `§1.3`).
- [x] 1.2 Robots have one coefficient, not rough, like a block's or a platform's. `RobotGroundFriction` and `RobotCarryFriction` become `RobotEngineFriction` (Claude, for the name: as `BlockEngineFriction` and `PlatformEngineFriction`). Not rough, the engine takes the smaller of a robot's and the other body's: at most the tiles' 1 on tiles, 0 under a slipping player, and the player's rough ground coefficient under an idle one. So the player runs off an idle robot as easily as off a driving one, and an idle player on a robot rides it as now.
- [x] 1.3 Robots don't slip: `RunAction.Run` takes whether the entity slips, true for the player and false for robots (Neil). A robot still drives with traction, a motor, so it stays a walker. Where slipping lives changed this to `Run` returning whether the entity should slip (§3.3).
- [~] 1.4 A robot that doesn't slip drives against its friction for as long as it walks, since its box slides along the floor (Claude):
  - It can't start unless its drive beats its coefficient times its weight. A robot weighs about 28.1 (mass 1, gravity 28.125) and drives at 11.25, so against tiles the coefficient must be under about 0.4, or the drive raised.
  - `RunAction.Run` fades the drive near top speed, so the robot settles where the faded drive equals friction: with 0.25, about 7, at about 1.1 tiles per second instead of 3.
  - Neil: raise the robot's drive and top speed to bring back about the same behavior. It differs only on a surface of friction 0, which is the player while it slips. There the player steadies the robot with a motor (§2), so it can't zoom off at more than its usual speed.
  - Tuning to be done, against the robot's patrol as it is now.
  - Found while laying out §3.4 (Claude): the friction a robot drives against is its coefficient times the normal force from the floor, which includes what it carries. The player's mass is 1, as a robot's is, so a robot carrying the player drives against twice the friction, and one carrying a block, of mass 2, three times. A loaded robot walks slower, and with enough load stalls and turns round. Now it slips, so only its drive and drag decide its speed, whatever it carries. Whether that is a problem or a feature is open; `RidingTest`'s patrolling robot will show it (§3.4).
- [x] 1.5 Robots get easier for other bodies to push, since a robot resists with its coefficient times its weight, which against tiles is at most 1. Neil: accepted. The player pushes robots fairly easily already: a robot turns round after a quarter of a second, driving with friction 0. And Neil has wanted idle robots standing on other entities to be easier to push.
- [ ] 1.6 Neil: shouldn't the player's coefficient also be 0 while it touches something at its left or right? In the air it is 0 already, so this is about the ground. A body's coefficient is for all its contacts, so 0 there takes away its grip on the floor too, as well as on the wall (Claude):
  - For: it answers the open question of a player pressed into a wall by something else, such as a robot, while riding a platform past it, which would hold the platform at the player's ground coefficient. Driving into a side the player already slips (`027 §6`), so driving changes nothing.
  - Against: an idle or braking player that touches anything at its side has no grip on what it stands on, only its traction, which an idle player doesn't apply. So a robot walking into an idle player pushes it along, where now it turns round against the player's grip (the design's open question says that the grip is far stronger than the robot's drive). An idle player beside a block or a robot on a platform slides on the platform as it speeds up or slows down, and so does what it carries, which grips its head at the smaller of the two coefficients, 0. A player braking against a robot that pushes it brakes with its drive alone.
  - The contacts are from the last step, and a side contact can come and go from tick to tick, as when standing against a wall, so the grip may flicker.
  - Options: (a) 0 at any side contact; (b) as now, 0 only in the air or while slipping, with driving into a side counting as slipping; (c) 0 at a side contact only while the player drives or brakes, not while it is idle, so that an idle player keeps its footing.
  - Claude leans (b) until something in play needs more: the case (a) fixes hasn't been seen in play, and (a) changes how the player is pushed everywhere.
  - Decision (Neil): (b).
- [x] 1.7 Carrying needn't be the player's only (Neil, after §2.1). With no carry friction anywhere, nothing is left that only a carrier does with friction: whatever stands on a body grips it at the smaller of their coefficients, or the larger rough one, as any two bodies do. So a robot carries a block, or a block a robot, just as a platform carries what rests on it, by plain friction. What stays the player's is steadying, which its tuning gives it and no other body's does (§2.2). This supersedes the first point of §1.1: no rule restricts carrying, and `CarryFriction` goes from every body, not only robots.

## 2. Carrying

- [x] 2.1 Decision (Neil): carry friction doesn't keep what the player carries on its head. What stands on the player has its own coefficient, so the rule that it takes its own while it touches something at its side (`027 §4`) goes too.
- [~] 2.2 The steadying motor holds what is carried instead, like a hand (Claude). On the ground, what is on the player's head then grips it with:
  - 0 while the player slips, driving or jumping: the smaller of the player's 0 and the carried body's own.
  - The player's rough ground coefficient while it is idle or brakes, 4. Holding a block through a reversal took about 5.6 of carry friction.
  - So `PlayerCarrySteadying`, 0 now (`027 §5.2`), goes up. It must stay below `PlayerGroundDriveForce`, so that a tunnel 2 tiles high still knocks a block off. Neil: to be tuned.
  - This may also answer the open question of a block left tipped after a reversal at speed, held by friction at its base, which suggested that friction at the base drop while the player steadies it (`019 §5.1`).
- [~] 2.3 In the air. Nothing is steadied in the air, so that jumping or falling can drop what is carried (`027 §5.4`). With carried bodies at their own coefficient, and the player at 0 in the air, a block would grip the head at 0 through a jump. Neil: might have to change; or could the player's coefficient in the air be its current carry coefficient? Claude:
  - With the player rough at 6 in the air, a block on its head grips it as it does now: today the block takes the player's 6, rough, and the player has 0. So carrying through a jump stays as it is. In free fall the block barely presses on the head, so friction holds little either way, which is part of why it drops now (`027 §5.4`).
  - But then the player isn't frictionless in the air, so it would grip walls. So it has 0 while it touches something at its side, as of the engine's last step, which makes "zero friction next to a wall" literal. The side may be another entity's, such as a robot's.
  - The contacts are from the last step, so on the tick the player reaches a wall it has 6 for that tick's step. The impact's normal force is large, so that one tick may take much of its vertical speed. To be measured, jumping into a wall and falling past a ledge's corner.
  - The ceiling is also a contact in the air, and the carried block is one. Bumping a tile ceiling would grip it at 6, taking horizontal speed. Contacts don't say what is at the ceiling, so 0 under a ceiling of tiles needs more than `Contacts` has. To be measured.
  - Other options: (b) steady in the air too, and let reversing hard, beyond the steadying force, be how a block drops in the air; (c) accept that a block slides over the head in the air.
  - If (a), the feature isn't about carrying any more: perhaps `PlayerAirFriction`.
  - Decision (Neil): try (c) first, without carry friction: the player keeps 0 in the air, as now. A player gripping a ceiling at 6 for even one tick is probably bad. How carrying through a jump feels is to be checked by playing.
- [x] 2.4 `CanBeCarried` stays, since it still says what the player steadies: not a platform (`025 §5.1`).
- [~] 2.5 A block stacked on a carried block now has its own coefficient against it, not the carrier's (`027 §5.1`). Tall stacks were fairly stable held by motors only at the bottom. Neil: assume it is fine for tall stacks to be less stable; to be checked by playing.

## 3. Where the code goes

- [x] 3.1 A `PlayerEntity`, a `WalkerEntity` that only the player is (Neil: we might have to make one, and might want to). Decision (Neil): as laid out below. It derives from `WalkerEntity` for the traction. Whether its rule also has 0 at a side contact is §1.6. It would be the natural place for the situational friction that applies to nothing else. Claude, laying it out:
  - What moves into it: `SetFrictionAfterEntitySteps`, and slipping, `SlipAfterEntitySteps` and its flag. Without the carrying branch, the player's rule is short: 0 while it slips or is in the air, else its ground coefficient, rough. The player's `EngineFriction` is already 0, so the rule can read as "its engine friction, except on the ground while it doesn't slip", with the engine friction as its coefficient in the air.
  - What stays in `Entity`: every other body's coefficient is its tuning's `EngineFriction`, all the time. It is put in the engine when the entity is built, and when the features change, by `UpdateFromFeatures`, which leaves it out now because `SetFrictionAfterEntitySteps` sets it every tick. `PlayerEntity` would override that, so as not to set its material twice.
  - Steadying stays in `Entity`. The carried body steadies itself with its own joint, reading its support's tuning, in its own step. Moving it into `PlayerEntity` would need either `Entity` to know about `PlayerEntity`, or the player to find what is on its head, which it doesn't know now.
  - `WalkerEntity` keeps traction, the animation properties and the controller, and loses its doc comment's account of friction. Robots are plain `WalkerEntity`s.
  - The player is built as a `PlayerEntity` in `WorldNode.CreatePlayer`, and `WorldNode.Player`, `PhaseAction.Player`, the player's controller and the carrying probes take one. It belongs in the entities subsystem, beside `WalkerEntity`: it is a kind of body, and its friction is how it meets other bodies, not behaviour. Its `intent.md` already names the player and friction, so it needn't change.
- [x] 3.2 Where the player's ground coefficient is read from. Decision (Neil): (b), for now. Only the player would have one, and `Entity` and `WalkerEntity` wouldn't read it. Options (Claude):
  - (a) `PlayerEntity` reads the feature `PlayerGroundFriction` itself, and `BodyTuning.GroundFriction` goes. But then the player's tuning no longer holds all of how its body meets other bodies, and a test can't give the player another ground coefficient through its body, as `NoDrag` does for drag, only through the features, as tests do now.
  - (b) `BodyTuning.GroundFriction` stays, and its doc comment says that only a player entity reads it. Every body's values stay in one place, as `029 §1.1` and `019 §6.1` have it. But it is a value that does nothing on any body but one. The steadying values already are, since only the player's tuning has them, but `Entity` reads those for any carrier.
  - (c) As (b), and `PlayerEntity` takes the tuning's ground coefficient as required: it throws if the tuning has none.
  - Claude leans (b): what a body is like stays in its tuning, as the steadying values do, which also only the player's tuning has.
- [x] 3.3 Slipping is the player's only, but `RunAction.Run` takes a `WalkerEntity` (§1.3), and so do the player's controller and `WalkerEntity.Controller`. Decision (Neil): (b), with `Run` returning whether the entity should slip, in place of §1.3's flag. Options (Claude):
  - (a) `SlipAfterEntitySteps` stays on `WalkerEntity`, and `Run`'s flag says whether to call it. Nothing else changes, but every walker can then be told to slip, and on anything but the player it would have to do nothing, or keep the machinery that §1.1 takes away.
  - (b) Slipping is on `PlayerEntity` only. `Run` returns whether the entity should slip, or takes an action to call, and the player's controller slips its entity. The controller then needs a `PlayerEntity`: it becomes an `ICharacterController<PlayerEntity>`, and `PlayerEntity` holds it, hiding `WalkerEntity.Controller`, and overrides `Control`, `ControlPaused` and `RequestsPause` to call it. `ICharacterController` is contravariant, so a controller for any walker still fits a player, but the hidden property is a wart: a player could be given a controller of each kind.
  - (c) As (b), but the controller moves out of `WalkerEntity`, into a generic base that every controlled kind of body shares, `ControlledEntity<TSelf>`, whose controller is an `ICharacterController<TSelf>`. No hiding, and `SimpleControlledEntity` could share it too. More machinery, for three kinds of body.
  - Claude leans (b), if `Run` returns whether to slip: the flag of §1.3 becomes its result, and only the player's controller acts on it.
- [x] 3.4 What changes in tests and probes (Claude, from reading them). Decision (Neil): as listed.
  - Changes because a name changes:
    - `BodiesTest.Colliding_bodies_share_their_momentum_without_bouncing` sets `RobotGroundFriction` to 0, which becomes `RobotEngineFriction`.
    - `CarryProbes.Describe` says "player" for any `WalkerEntity` support, which robots are too: it becomes `PlayerEntity`. The carrying probes' `WalkerEntity player` parameters may become `PlayerEntity`, but needn't.
  - May fail, because what they test now rests on different friction:
    - `BodiesTest.A_block_that_lands_on_the_players_head_stays_there_while_the_player_runs`. The landing's catch and the block staying put to 0.05 tiles came from the player's carry coefficient. Now the block grips the running player at 0, so steadying alone holds it, and the test is where `PlayerCarrySteadying` gets tuned (§2.2). Its last part, stopping without throwing the block, rests on the player's ground coefficient, 4 instead of 6, and steadying.
    - `RidingTest.An_idle_player_stays_where_it_stands_on_a_patrolling_robot`. The player stands on the robot at its rough 4, as now, but the robot drives against twice its friction (§1.4). It asserts the robot covers more than 67 tiles in 30 seconds, so it checks the retuned drive under load. `A_jump_from_a_robot_keeps_the_robots_speed` needs the robot moving at more than 1 tile per second.
    - `BodiesTest.A_running_player_pushes_a_robot_faster_than_it_walks` and `A_robot_that_walks_into_an_idle_player_turns_round`. The robot no longer slips while driving, and its drive is raised. The second asserts that the idle player isn't moved, which the player's grip, 4 times its weight, should still hold against a raised drive. To be seen.
    - `PhaseTest.RunningAtStackedRobotsGrabsTheBottomOne`: the top robot stands on the bottom one at the robots' own coefficient instead of 6.
  - Unaffected, as far as reading shows: the player's own tests in `PlayerControllerTest`, including that it has no friction in the air and that friction stops it without reversing, since the player's rule doesn't change (§1.6).
  - New, kept few:
    - The case that started this: the player runs off an idle robot about as quickly as off tiles. A robot boxed in between two walls stands still (`_move` 0), with the player on it.
    - Perhaps: a block on an idle robot stays on it, at its own coefficient. Probably covered by playing.
  - Probes to compare before and after, run on this branch before the change: `ride-robot`, `ride-platform`, `run-off`, `push`, `carry`, `carry-checks`, `wobble`, `offset-landing`, `tilted`, `landing`, `buffered-landing`, `player`, `phase`. Those about carrying will differ by design; the rest should print about what they did before.
