# 054: Scoped ticks

## Context

Inciting event (2026-11-07): deliberating scripts for entities (`056`), Neil wanted a script about an entity to end when the entity is removed, through its `using` and `finally` blocks, while the entity is still whole. Until now only stopping a world's scheduler cancels what runs on it (`053`). This record began as section 3 of `056`, and was split out so that the scheduler's work could land on its own; its items keep the substance they had there.

Goals:
- A run can be cut short by something other than its world being dropped, and unwinds as it does then.
- A chain of tasks still runs to its end in one tick (`043 §2.6`), whichever runs it crosses.
- The scheduler doesn't mirror the scene's tree (Neil).

Anti-goals:
- Cancelling single runs on request, which `053 §4.1` decided against.

Closed on 2026-11-07. Outcomes are in the architecture doc of `Tasks`.

## 1. Scopes

- [x] 1.1 Whether a run can be scoped to an entity, so that it is cancelled when the entity is removed. This reopens `053 §4.1` narrowly: nothing asks for the cancelling but the entity's going, and what is cancelled is only what runs on the entity's tick. Decision (Neil): yes, through scopes (§1.4): what ends is a scope, and only what waits on it is cancelled; nothing asks for a run to be cancelled, so `053 §4.1` stands for requests.
- [x] 1.2 How an entity's tick relates to the world's. Every async method's task takes the scheduler of the first unfinished task it awaits, and one that then awaits a task of another scheduler fails (`TickTask.TakeScheduler`): a chain runs on one scheduler. Options:
  - (a) A tree of scopes (Claude, first): `TickScheduler` is the root, which alone runs, holds the one queue and the clock, and each entity's tick is a child scope of it, which only tags its waits. Stopping a scope cancels the waits of its subtree, and the root's run unwinds what they resume. Every task's continuations go to the root's queue, so a chain can cross scopes, and still runs to its end in one tick (`043 §2.6`). The world's `Stop` covers every scope. The cost is a scope type, and a tree in the scheduler that mirrors the scene's.
  - (b) Independent schedulers (Neil): the world and every entity each have their own `TickScheduler`, unchanged. `WorldNode.StopAndQueueFree` calls each entity's `StopAndQueueFree`, which stops its scheduler. An entity's `Time` is its lifetime. Questions this raises (Claude):
    - Where each entity's scheduler runs. In `Entity.Step` it would resume tasks partway through the entity steps, with some entities stepped and some not, and changes to bodies seen by the controllers that step after, which the tick's order rules out (`022 §2.1`, `046 §1.2`). So the world runs them all at its scheduler's point in the tick, in the order of its entities.
    - Chains that cross schedulers. A method that awaits both `world.Tick` and `target.Tick` fails, and so does a `Sequence` whose steps run on different entities' ticks. They need a bridge, such as a `While` that polls the other task until it has settled, which can add a tick per crossing, depending on the order in which the schedulers run, unless the world reruns them all until none has anything queued. That needs a way to run a scheduler's queue without advancing its clock: a change to `TickScheduler` after all, though a small one.
    - Lifting the one-scheduler rule instead is unsafe: a method's task whose scheduler is an entity's that has since stopped queues its awaiters where nothing runs them, and they never resume.
  - (c) One scheduler with flat scopes (Claude, after (b)): (a) without nesting. The world's `TickScheduler` keeps the one queue and the clock, and makes a scope for each entity, which only tags its waits, with no scopes of scopes. Keeps (a)'s single chain, and (b)'s flatness: the scheduler doesn't mirror the scene's tree. An entity's lifetime, if a script wants it, is the scheduler's `Time` less the scope's start.
  - Superseded by §1.4, which keeps (c)'s one scheduler and flatness, with no tick per entity.
- [x] 1.3 The interface for scopes, under `056 §3.8` (Neil asked; Claude's sketch). Tasks knows nothing of entities, so a scope can't be linked to an entity, only to another tick: an entity's tick stands for its lifetime.
  - `ITick.CreateScope(params ITick[] alsoEndsWith)` returns an `ITickScope`, an `ITick` that is also `IDisposable`, whose lifetime is the intersection of this tick's and those of `alsoEndsWith`: it is stopped when any of them is. With no arguments, it is a child of this tick, as in (a) of §1.2; with several, it is linked to each, which the scene's tree couldn't say.
  - A scope is a thin `ITick` over its scheduler: its waits are the scheduler's, tagged with the scope, and every task's scheduler is still the `TickScheduler`, so the one-scheduler rule of §1.2 holds and chains cross scopes freely. `Time` and `Dt` are the scheduler's. `While`, `All` and the rest are written on `NextTick` and `Wait`, so a scope gets them for nothing. All the scopes made from it, or linked to it, must share a scheduler.
  - Stopping a scope cancels its waits and stops the scopes made from it or linked to it; a wait made on a stopped scope is cancelled at once, as now. Whoever made it holds the `ITickScope` and stops it, through `Dispose`; whoever is only given its `ITick` can't. Disposing it at the end of a run, with `using`, also unlinks it from longer-lived scopes, so that a short run linked to a long-lived entity leaves nothing behind.
  - An entity's scope is made from the world's tick by the world, and stopped when it removes the entity (`056 §3.5`). The `ScriptRunner` makes each run's scope, linked to its linked entity parameters (`056 §3.8`), and gives it to the script as its world's `Tick`.
  - Superseded by §1.4, at Neil's prompting, before it was built.
- [x] 1.4 Scopes ended by a condition (Neil): `CreateScope` takes a `Func<bool>` that holds until the scope is to end. Decision (Neil): this, over §1.2 and §1.3, with the convention on conditions below. Built as `ITick.Scoped` (Claude): `TickScheduler` is no longer an `ITick` itself, but has one, its `Tick`, with no scope, so that a `ScopedTick` is the one implementation of `ITick`, and no other has to forward to it. Claude's working of it:
  - `ITick.Scope(Func<bool> condition)` returns an `ITick` whose waits are the scheduler's, each recorded with the scope's condition. Each run, before it finishes any wait, the scheduler cancels the waits whose condition no longer holds, and the run unwinds what they resume, as `Stop` does. A wait made while the condition doesn't hold is cancelled at once. A scope made from a scope ends with either: its condition is its parent's, then its own, so a parent's guard, such as that an entity is still there, is checked before the child's condition reads the entity.
  - Nothing is registered, linked or disposed: a scope is only a value that tags its waits. A scope nothing waits on costs nothing, and one forgotten costs nothing, so there is no `ITickScope` and no `using`. The links, the cascades and the unlinking of §1.3 go.
  - It is not the cooperative check that `053 §1.1` rejected: the scheduler checks, not the method, and the method unwinds by the same exception, with nothing to remember.
  - An entity needs no tick of its own: it has a flag, such as `IsRemoved`, and what is about it makes `world.Tick.Scope(() => !entity.IsRemoved)`. A script about two entities ands their flags; the conversation runs on the world's tick and reads them. The flag is a managed field, so a condition can read it safely even of an entity already freed.
  - The order in the tick (`056 §3.5`): the world sets the flag at its fixed point for removals, before its scheduler runs, and frees the entity after, so that what the scheduler unwinds finds it whole. An entity removed partway through a run is noticed at the next run's start; anything of its scope already queued resumes in between, with the entity still whole, since it isn't freed until after that next run.
  - Other conditions come for nothing: a deadline, `() => tick.Time < deadline`, is a timeout that cancels what it times out, which `ITick.Any` has been waiting for (Tasks' doc); and an important cutscene can end what runs on a scope whose condition is that no such cutscene has started (`056 §3.7`).
  - A condition must be cheap and have no side effects, and one that throws fails the tick (agreed by Neil); `055 §3` later sent it to a sink instead. What an awaiter sees when a scope cancels what it awaits is for `056 §3.6`.
